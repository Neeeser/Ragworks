# Meridian Shelf Survey

Instrumented survey of the Meridian shelf, seasons one to three. Prepared as the design basis for the anchor array.

## Purpose and scope

This report records the three-season instrumented survey of the Meridian shelf, undertaken to fix the anchor design for a fixed offshore platform. It covers site selection, the anchor array as installed, the loading anomaly recorded in the first winter, the instrumentation revision that followed, and the revised design case that came out of it.

The scope deliberately excludes the topside structure and the export cable beyond the shelf edge, both of which are covered by separate reports. Where a figure in this report differs from one in the preliminary desk study, the value here supersedes it: the desk study was built on regional hindcast data, and every section below is built on measurements taken at the site.

## Site selection

Eleven candidate sites on the northern shelf were ranked against three controlling criteria: substrate stability, tidal exposure, and distance from the shipping lane. Substrate stability was assessed from cores taken on a two hundred metre grid; tidal exposure from a six-week current meter deployment at each site; separation from the lane from the published traffic separation scheme.

Three sites cleared every threshold. Of those, only Meridian offered a continuous bedrock shelf wide enough to take the anchor array without straddling the sediment margin, and it was selected on that basis. The two rejected sites both required the array to bridge bedrock and sediment, which would have made differential settlement the governing design case rather than hydrodynamic loading.

## The anchor array as installed

The array consists of nine piles driven to refusal into the bedrock shelf, arranged as a central pile with two concentric rings of four. Refusal depth varied between 6.2 and 9.8 metres, consistent with the bedrock profile the cores predicted.

Each pile carries a strain gauge at the mudline and a second at mid-length. The original installation sampled both at four hertz and logged five-minute maxima, a rate chosen on the assumption that loading would be dominated by the tidal cycle and the wave spectrum, neither of which needed finer resolution to characterise.

## First-winter loading anomaly

During the first winter the outer ring of three piles on the eastern side recorded loads up to forty percent above the design case. The exceedances were not isolated: they recurred on eleven separate occasions across January and February, each lasting between forty minutes and two hours.

The initial review considered and rejected three explanations. Gauge drift was excluded because the inner piles, on the same logger and calibration, recorded nothing unusual. Vessel impact was excluded from the traffic record. Scour was excluded by the February dive inspection, which found the seabed profile within tolerance at every pile.

## The standing wave

The accepted explanation is a standing wave that forms when the flood tide meets the wave reflected off the eastern spur. The two wave trains are close enough in period that the superposition is near-resonant, and the antinode sits over the eastern side of the array.

It appears only between the third and fifth hour of the flood, and only when the wind has been from the north-east for more than about twenty-four hours — long enough to build the incident sea the spur reflects. Because both conditions must hold together, and the original site survey ran for six weeks in a season when north-easterlies are uncommon, the survey never observed it. This is the central finding of the report.

## Instrumentation revision

Two pressure transducers were installed on the eastern spur specifically to capture the reflected wave at source, so the standing wave can be predicted from the incident conditions rather than inferred after the fact from the strain record.

The strain sampling rate was raised from four to sixteen hertz across all nine piles, and the logger was changed to retain full time series rather than five-minute maxima during any period when the spur transducers indicate the reflecting condition. The maxima logging is retained at all other times to keep the data volume manageable.

## Revised design loads

The revised loads treat the standing wave as a design case rather than an anomaly. The governing combination is now the near-resonant superposition at mean high water springs with a twenty-four hour north-easterly fetch, which produces a load on the outer eastern piles about 1.45 times the previously governing case.

The outer piles were sleeved over their upper four metres and the array's rated capacity was reduced by fifteen percent. Both measures were complete before the second winter. No further exceedances have been recorded in the two winters since.

## Sediment transport

The sand wedge on the western approach migrates seasonally, advancing east through the winter and retreating through the summer. Its leading edge moved 34 metres between the first and second survey and 29 metres between the second and third, so the rate is treated as roughly constant at 30 metres per season.

The wedge does not reach the anchor array and is not expected to within the design life. It does reach the planned cable corridor, which is why the corridor was realigned; see the following section.

## Cable routing

The trench alignment was moved 180 metres north of the preliminary route so that it clears the sand wedge at its furthest recorded advance, with a further 90 metres of margin for the constant-rate assumption above.

The realignment adds 240 metres of cable and one additional joint. That cost was accepted in preference to burying the cable deeper on the original line, because a cable under a migrating wedge cannot be inspected or recovered without first moving the sediment above it.

## Corrosion allowance

Sacrificial anode mass was sized for a twenty-five year service life against the measured seawater resistivity and the recorded temperature range, both of which sit within the values assumed at design stage.

The sleeving fitted to the outer piles adds surface area that the original anode calculation did not carry. The anode mass was recomputed and increased by eleven percent at the same time the sleeves were fitted, so the service life is unchanged.

## Monitoring plan

Monitoring has two parts. Continuous strain telemetry runs on all nine piles, with the conditional full-rate logging described above; the spur transducers feed the same logger and are what trigger it.

A dive inspection is carried out quarterly, timed so that one of the four falls within the winter period when the standing wave is possible. The inspection covers scour depth at each pile, sleeve condition, and anode consumption.

## Conclusions and programme impact

The controlling conclusion is that a six-week survey window is too short for any site whose governing load depends on a coincidence of tide state and wind direction. At Meridian both conditions are individually common and their coincidence is not, so a short survey is likely to miss it entirely rather than merely under-sample it.

The recommendation is a full annual measurement cycle before the anchor design is fixed. For a programme of this shape that adds roughly nine months before the design freeze. Against that, the retrofit actually carried out here — sleeving, recomputed anodes, a revised logger regime, and a fifteen percent capacity reduction on an array already installed — cost substantially more than nine months of survey would have.
